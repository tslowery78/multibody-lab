How many ways can this thing move?

Grübler's equation (extended by Kutzbach) counts a mechanism's degrees of freedom from nothing but a parts list — before you write a single equation of motion.

M = 3(L−1) − 2J1 − J2

01What it's for

Before you build a stiffness matrix or run Adams, you want one number: how many independent inputs does this linkage need before its configuration is fully determined? That number is the mobility, M — also called the degrees of freedom, DOF.

Grübler's insight (1917, generalized by Kutzbach in 1929) is that you don't need the geometry to get M — just a count of rigid bodies and how they're connected. Free-floating in a plane, every rigid body has 3 DOF (x, y, rotation). Bolt it to the ground and it loses all 3. Pin two bodies together with a revolute or prismatic joint and you remove 2 of the 3 relative DOF between them, leaving 1. A joint that only removes 1 DOF (a cam-follower or roller contact, leaving 2 free) is called a half joint.

02The planar formula

Ground counts as one of the links. J1 = full joints (revolute, prismatic — remove 2 DOF each). J2 = half joints (rolling/sliding contact — remove 1 DOF each).

SymbolMeaning
Lnumber of links, including the fixed frame/ground
J1number of 1-DOF-remaining ("full") joints — pin, slider
J2number of 2-DOF-remaining ("half") joints — cam, roller
Mmobility — independent inputs needed to fix the configuration

03Watch it: the four-bar linkage

4 links (including ground), 4 revolute joints, no half joints. Drag the crank or press play — notice that one input angle (the crank) fixes every other link's position. That's M = 1 in action.

L = 4 J1 = 4 J2 = 0 M = 3(4−1) − 2(4) − 0 = 1

04Same count, different shape: slider-crank

Swap the rocker for a block sliding in a straight channel. Still 4 links and 4 full joints (3 revolute + 1 prismatic) — Grübler doesn't care that one "joint" is a slider instead of a pin. Same M = 1. This is the mechanism inside every piston engine and reciprocating compressor.

L = 4 J1 = 4 (3 pin + 1 slider) J2 = 0 M = 3(4−1) − 2(4) − 0 = 1

05Try it — planar mobility calculator

Punch in your own link and joint counts, or load a preset. Watch M flip sign: 0 is a rigid structure (a truss), negative means overconstrained (redundant members), positive means it moves with that many independent inputs.

M = 3(L−1) − 2J1 − J2 = 1

06Going 3D: the general (Kutzbach) form

In space, a free rigid body has 6 DOF, not 3. Every joint now removes 6−f constraints, where f is however much relative motion that joint type still allows.

M = 6(L−1) − Σ(6 − fi)
Jointf (DOF it allows)6−f (constraints removed)
Revolute / prismatic15
Cylindrical / universal24
Spherical (ball joint) / planar33

Set every joint to a revolute (f=1) and every body to a plane (drop 3 DOF/link and 3 constraints/joint instead of 6) and this collapses back to the planar formula in §02 — it's the same idea, just fewer dimensions per body.

Where it breaks: both formulas assume every joint constraint is independent. Special geometry can make constraints redundant, so the real mechanism moves even when the formula says it's locked. The textbook case is Bennett's linkage — four links, four revolute joints in space:
M = 6(4−1) − 5(4) = 18 − 20 = −2
predicting an overconstrained structure. Built with Bennett's specific link-length and twist-angle ratios, it is in fact a 1-DOF mechanism. The formula is a fast necessary check, not a proof — it's the "order of magnitude" pass before you trust a mechanism (or a rover suspension linkage) to move the way the parts count says it should.
